\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Owner: Dan + Dat, together, 25/09.

Backdoor repair is not the end of a model's lifecycle; the repaired
model still has to be distributed, cached, and served. Edge inference
deployments increasingly partition a model into independently signed
shards so that workers can cache, update, and verify them separately
during a rolling update, rather than replacing the entire model
atomically. This partitioning is attractive precisely because it makes
rollout incremental: a repair can reach some workers before others,
and an interrupted or partial rollout is treated as an ordinary
operational condition rather than a failure. Existing supply-chain
protections for this setting verify that each shard is authentically
signed, but per-shard signature verification proves only that a shard
is a genuine artifact, not that it belongs to the same repair epoch as
the shards around it. An adversary who controls the artifact cache or
the update distribution path -- without forging any signature or
compromising the loader -- can exploit exactly this gap, supplying a
validly signed, pre-repair shard to a worker during an interrupted
rollout and causing it to serve a hybrid model that mixes repaired and
stale weights.

We measure the ML-specific consequence of this gap rather than propose
a new protocol. Using BadNets on CIFAR-10 with PreActResNet18,
partitioned into six shards, we ask when a hybrid model assembled this
way restores backdoor behavior, and whether that restoration is
visible to standard utility monitoring. The answer depends sharply on
the repair method. Under global clean fine-tuning, rolling back any
single shard is weak: the highest single-shard attack success rate
(ASR) we observe is 8.94\%, and the repair is distributed across
shards in a way that no individual shard can undo. This weakness is
not benign, however, because it is also silent -- clean accuracy (CA)
stays above 86\% in every hybrid configuration we test, so utility
monitoring gives a defender no signal that a stale shard has been
accepted. We report this single-shard result plainly as the lower
bound of the attack, not as its headline, and place its practical
implication first: detection has to happen at the artifact layer,
where shard provenance is checked, not at the inference layer, where
only aggregate accuracy is visible. The real attack surface appears
once an adversary substitutes multiple shards. A repair-aware attacker
who targets the shards carrying the largest share of the repair update
crosses a sharp threshold at three substituted shards, restoring

The defense we evaluate is deliberately not novel. Epoch-manifest
pinning -- binding each shard to a per-epoch identifier in signed
snapshot metadata, so that a loader can reject a shard as
epoch-mismatched rather than accept it as individually valid -- is
already specified in update frameworks such as TUF~\cite{tuf} and

contribution is to show that this gap exists, unaddressed, in
model-shard distribution, and to measure what closing it costs: our
serving comparison (Section~\ref{sec:serving}) finds 34 additional
bytes of manifest overhead and no measurable per-load verification
cost, making adoption a low-cost baseline control rather than a
design trade-off.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

\textbf{Setting.} A model is partitioned into $n$ contiguous shards that
are hosted by different edge nodes (split or partitioned
inference~\cite{kang2017neurosurgeon}), a deployment pattern envisioned for
6G edge intelligence~\cite{letaief2022edgeai6g}. Each shard is signed
individually and delivered through an artifact cache, and each node
verifies only the shard it hosts. Repairs are rolled out in stages, so
an interrupted rollout caused by a link outage or handover is a normal
operating condition.

\textbf{Adversary.} The adversary controls only the artifact cache or
update path. Its goal is to make the deployment serve a \emph{hybrid}
model that mixes repaired shards with validly signed pre-repair shards,
restoring the backdoor without failing any per-shard check. It knows the
architecture and partition boundaries and holds both signed versions of
every shard, which any party that cached the pre-repair artifacts has.
It can therefore compute, offline, how much of the repair each shard
carries (Section~\ref{sec:share}). It needs no queries to the model and
no training data.

\textbf{Trust assumptions.} The coordinator, loader, runtime and any
stored minimum-epoch state are trusted; signatures cannot be forged.
A fully compromised worker that misreports its loaded weights is out of
scope, since it requires attestation.

Per-shard verification proves that each shard is authentic, not that
all shards belong to the same epoch. Epoch-manifest pinning, as defined
in TUF~\cite{tuf} and Uptane~\cite{uptane}, closes this gap without a new
protocol. We measure the ML-specific consequence of its absence.

\subsection{Models, Attack, and Repairs}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Data, model and attack.} We use CIFAR-10~\cite{krizhevsky2009cifar}
(50{,}000 training and 10{,}000 test images) and
PreActResNet18~\cite{he2016identity}. The infected model is the publicly
released BackdoorBench~\cite{backdoorbench2024} BadNets~\cite{gu2019badnets}
checkpoint for this pair (poisoning ratio 10\%, 3$\times$3 white patch in the
bottom-right corner, target class~0); we do not retrain it. On our
evaluation pipeline it reaches CA~$=$~89.41\% and ASR~$=$~97.43\%.

\textbf{Repair~(a): global clean fine-tuning.} The infected model is
fine-tuned for 20 epochs on the full clean training set (SGD, momentum 0.9,
weight decay $5\!\times\!10^{-4}$, learning rate 0.01 with cosine annealing,
batch 128, seed 42), updating every parameter. This yields CA~$=$~93.35\%
and ASR~$=$~0.90\%. Giving the defender the full clean training set makes
this a strong, global repair; CA even rises by 3.94 points.
\ifincludeanp

\textbf{Repair~(b): Adversarial Neuron Pruning (ANP).} We apply
ANP~\cite{wu2021anp} to the same infected checkpoint with BackdoorBench's
reference implementation and default CIFAR-10 settings
(Table~\ref{tab:anp_config}). ANP learns a mask score per BatchNorm
neuron on 5\% of the clean training data and prunes (zeroes) every neuron

every other parameter is bit-identical to the infected model, so the two

\subsection{Partitioning and Substitution}
\label{sec:partition}

We partition PreActResNet18 into $n$ contiguous shards along
top-level module boundaries. At $n\!=\!6$ the shards are the stem
(\texttt{conv1}), the four residual stages \texttt{layer1}--\texttt{layer4},
and the classifier head (\texttt{linear}), indexed 0--5. At $n\!=\!3$ they
are \{stem, \texttt{layer1}\}, \{\texttt{layer2}, \texttt{layer3}\} and
\{\texttt{layer4}, head\}. Rolling back a set of shards replaces all of
their tensors, including BatchNorm statistics, with the infected
versions and leaves every other shard repaired. Rolling back all $n$
shards reproduces the infected model exactly (ASR~$=$~97.43\%,
CA~$=$~89.41\% at both granularities), which verifies the harness.

\subsection{Repair-Share Metric}
\label{sec:share}

The \emph{repair share} of shard $s$ is the fraction of the squared
repair update that it carries:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{share}_s \;=\; \frac{\|\,\delta_s\,\|_2^2}{\sum_j \|\,\delta_j\,\|_2^2},
  \qquad
  \delta_s \;=\; \theta^{\mathrm{rep}}_s - \theta^{\mathrm{inf}}_s,
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_s$ are the learnable parameters of shard $s$; BatchNorm
buffers (\texttt{running\_mean}, \texttt{running\_var},
\texttt{num\_batches\_tracked}) are excluded because they are not
learned. By construction $\sum_s \mathrm{share}_s = 1$, and the share of
a set of shards is the sum of their shares. The metric needs only the two
signed checkpoints, so an attacker who holds both can compute it offline.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\textbf{ASR} is the fraction of triggered test images whose true class is
not the target that are classified as the target class
(9{,}000 images), following BackdoorBench~\cite{backdoorbench2024}.
\textbf{CA} is top-1 accuracy on the 10{,}000 clean test images. Both are
measured in evaluation mode. With these test-set sizes, the 95\%
binomial confidence half-width is at most 1.0 percentage point for
either metric, so we report differences below 1 point as ties.

\subsection{Determinism and Sampling Variance}

The repair-aware and architectural orders are deterministic, and
re-running the sweep under a second evaluation seed (0 instead of 42)
reproduced all 49 repeated subset evaluations exactly, so the pipeline has
no hidden randomness. This is a determinism check, not a replication over
repair seeds. Only the random strategy depends on the seed: its mean ASR at
$k=3$ over five draws is 17.93\% under seed~42 and 44.07\% under seed~0,

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{One stale shard is not enough; a few are.} Under a strong global
repair, a single rolled-back shard restores at most 8.94\% ASR, so the
single-shard case is the lower bound of the attack. Recovery is a
threshold effect driven by an interaction between shards: the backdoor
returns once \texttt{layer2} and \texttt{layer4} are both stale. An
attacker holding both signed epochs can find these shards offline from
the repair share, and needs three stale shards to restore 92.89\% ASR;
an attacker who knows only the partition needs five.

\textbf{Utility monitoring is a weak signal.} The stealthiest successful
hybrid loses 5.97 points of CA against the repaired model but stays
within 2.03 points of the infected model that was deployed before the
repair. Detecting it therefore requires labelled canary data and a
tight alarm threshold; label-free monitoring would not see it. Other
successful hybrids are cruder (e.g.\ $\{2,4\}$ at 76.99\% CA) and would be
caught.

\textbf{The fix is cheap.} All hybrids share one root cause: each shard
is checked for authenticity, not for membership in the same epoch.
Binding every shard to an epoch identifier in signed snapshot metadata,
as TUF~\cite{tuf} and Uptane~\cite{uptane} specify, rejected every skewed
load in our serving test for 34 extra bytes per manifest. In partitioned
edge inference each node sees only its own shard, so the epoch check must
be enforced per node against a pinned manifest, not only at the
publisher.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Backdoor repair and its fragility.} Fine-tuning and pruning
defenses such as fine-pruning~\cite{liu2018fineprune} and
ANP~\cite{wu2021anp} remove backdoors after training. Min et
al.~\cite{min2024uncovering} show that models purified by several such
%%Hung: 'BAU, SAM, FST and BTI' are undefined acronyms. Either expand them or write 'several purification defenses'.
defenses (including ANP, BAU, SAM, FST and BTI) quickly re-learn the
backdoor when retuned on a few poisoned samples or probed with crafted
queries. DeferBad~\cite{shin2024deferbad} designs the backdoor so that
unlearning leaves a residue that later benign fine-tuning reactivates.
Both need training or queries. Our attacker needs neither: it restores
backdoor behaviour purely by serving shards that were once legitimately
signed.

\textbf{Weight substitution and merging.} The subnet replacement
attack~\cite{qi2022subnet} overwrites part of a deployed model with
weights trained for the purpose, and BadMerging~\cite{zhang2024badmerging}
plants backdoors through merging of distinct models. Our attacker cannot
craft weights; it reuses the two endpoints of one repair.

\textbf{Artifact integrity and update security.}
Sentry~\cite{gan2025sentry} authenticates ML artifacts as they are loaded,
and the OpenSSF Model Signing specification~\cite{oms2025} signs one
manifest of per-file digests. Both prove that each artifact is authentic.
When shards are verified independently by the nodes that host them, as in
partitioned edge inference~\cite{kang2017neurosurgeon,letaief2022edgeai6g},
authenticity alone does not stop a node from accepting a stale but genuine
shard. TUF~\cite{tuf} and Uptane~\cite{uptane} define the rollback and
mix-and-match protections that close this gap; we quantify what their
absence costs for a repaired model.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Signed but stale shards can undo a backdoor repair in partitioned edge
inference. Against a strong fine-tuning repair, one stale shard restores
at most 8.94\% ASR, but three shards chosen from the two signed
checkpoints restore 92.89\% ASR at 87.38\% CA, and recovery hinges on
\texttt{layer2} and \texttt{layer4} being stale together.

Epoch-manifest pinning rejects these hybrids for 34 bytes per manifest and
should be a baseline control wherever 6G edge nodes receive model shards
through caches.
