\begin{abstract}
Edge intelligence in 6G networks distributes models to edge nodes as
independently signed shards that are cached and updated in stages over
intermittent links. A per-shard signature proves that a shard is authentic,
not that every shard belongs to the same model version. We measure what this
gap costs after a backdoor has been repaired, when an adversary who controls
the artifact cache re-introduces validly signed pre-repair shards into an
otherwise repaired model. Using BadNets on CIFAR-10 with PreActResNet18
partitioned into six shards, we evaluate all 63 rollback subsets against two
independently fine-tuned repairs. An adversary who ranks shards by repair
share restores 92.3\% and 95.8\% attack success rate (ASR) with four stale
shards while clean accuracy stays within 2.3 points of the repaired model;
one stale shard restores at most 16.4\%. Recovery depends on which shards are
stale rather than on how much of the repair they carry: every hybrid above
50\% ASR contains \texttt{layer4}, whereas the shard holding 48\% of the
repair update is dispensable, and the two repairs agree on the 50\%
classification of 60 of 64 subsets. Epoch-manifest pinning, as specified by
TUF and Uptane, rejects every skewed load for 34 bytes per manifest and no
measurable verification overhead.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Rewritten 26/09 by Hung from results/analysis/2026-09-26_hung_digest.md.

Backdoor repair does not end a model's lifecycle, for the repaired model must
still be distributed, cached, and served. In 6G edge intelligence a model is
increasingly partitioned into independently signed shards, so that edge nodes
can fetch, cache, and verify each shard separately over intermittent wireless
links, and a repair can be rolled out in stages rather than as one atomic
replacement~\cite{kang2017neurosurgeon,letaief2022edgeai6g}. Staged rollout is
attractive precisely because it tolerates interruption: a link outage or a
handover leaves some nodes on the new version and others on the old, and the
deployment continues to serve. Supply-chain protections for this setting verify
that each shard is authentically signed. A per-shard signature, however, proves
only that a shard is a genuine artifact; it does not prove that the shard
belongs to the same repair epoch as the shards around it. An adversary who
controls the artifact cache or the update path, without forging a signature or
compromising a loader, can supply a validly signed pre-repair shard to a node
during an interrupted rollout, and the node then serves a hybrid model that
mixes repaired and stale weights.

We measure the machine-learning consequence of this gap rather than propose a
protocol. Using BadNets on CIFAR-10 with PreActResNet18 partitioned into six
shards, we repair the infected model by clean fine-tuning under two seeds,
evaluate every one of the 63 possible rollback subsets against each repair, and
ask when a hybrid restores backdoor behaviour and whether utility monitoring
would notice. Three findings follow. An adversary who holds both signed
versions can rank shards by repair share offline; four shards chosen this way
restore 92.3\% and 95.8\% attack success rate (ASR) under the two repairs, at
a clean accuracy (CA) within 2.3 points of the repaired model and within 0.2
points of the infected model that was in service before the repair. Recovery
is governed by which shards are stale rather than by how much of the repair
they carry: every hybrid above 50\% ASR contains \texttt{layer4}, whereas
\texttt{layer3}, which holds 48\% of the repair update, is dispensable, and
the repair share covered by a subset predicts its ASR only weakly (Spearman
$\rho \le 0.41$). Conversely, a single stale shard is a weak attack: the
largest single-shard ASR is 16.4\%, and the CA of every single-shard hybrid
stays within 6.8 points of the repaired model, so a stale shard is accepted
silently and to little effect. The two repairs agree on whether a subset
exceeds 50\% ASR in 60 of 64 cases.

\noindent Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We formulate shard-version skew as a supply-chain attack on a repaired, partitioned model, in which an adversary who controls only the artifact cache serves validly signed pre-repair shards (Section~\ref{sec:threat}).
\item We evaluate all 63 rollback subsets against two independent fine-tuning repairs and show that four shards ranked by repair share restore 92.3\% and 95.8\% ASR at a CA within 2.3 points of the repaired model (Sections~\ref{sec:kcurve} and~\ref{sec:strategy}).
\item We show that recovery is governed by shard identity rather than by repair share: every hybrid above 50\% ASR contains \texttt{layer4}, and the two repairs agree on 60 of 64 subsets (Sections~\ref{sec:locality} and~\ref{sec:variance}).
\item We measure that epoch-manifest pinning rejects every skewed load for 34 bytes per manifest and no measurable verification cost (Section~\ref{sec:serving}).
\end{itemize}

The defense we evaluate is deliberately not new. Epoch-manifest pinning binds
every shard to a per-epoch identifier in signed snapshot metadata, so that a
loader rejects a shard as epoch-mismatched instead of accepting it as
individually valid; TUF~\cite{tuf} and Uptane~\cite{uptane} specify it for
exactly this class of problem. Our contribution is to show that the gap it
closes is open in model-shard distribution and to measure what closing it
costs: in our serving comparison (Section~\ref{sec:serving}) it adds 34 bytes
of manifest per epoch and no measurable per-load verification cost, which makes
it a baseline control rather than a design trade-off.

Section~\ref{sec:threat} states the threat model. Section~\ref{sec:setup}
describes the dataset, architecture, attack, repairs, and partitioning.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the single-shard, multi-shard, and
shard-selection results for both repairs, \ifincludeanp the contrast with a
local repair, \fi the determinism and replication checks, and the serving
comparison. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the results and states
their limits, Section~\ref{sec:related} relates them to prior work, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

\textbf{Setting.} A model is partitioned into $n$ contiguous shards that
are hosted by different edge nodes (split or partitioned
inference~\cite{kang2017neurosurgeon}), a deployment pattern envisioned for
6G edge intelligence~\cite{letaief2022edgeai6g}. Each shard is signed
individually and delivered through an artifact cache, and each node
verifies only the shard it hosts. Repairs are rolled out in stages, so
an interrupted rollout caused by a link outage or handover is a normal
operating condition.

\textbf{Adversary.} The adversary controls only the artifact cache or
update path. Its goal is to make the deployment serve a \emph{hybrid}
model that mixes repaired shards with validly signed pre-repair shards,
restoring the backdoor without failing any per-shard check. It knows the
architecture and partition boundaries and holds both signed versions of
every shard, which any party that cached the pre-repair artifacts has.
It can therefore compute, offline, how much of the repair each shard
carries (Section~\ref{sec:share}). It needs no queries to the model and
no training data.

\subsection{Partitioning and Substitution}
\label{sec:partition}

We partition PreActResNet18 into $n$ contiguous shards along
top-level module boundaries. At $n\!=\!6$ the shards are the stem
(\texttt{conv1}), the four residual stages \texttt{layer1}--\texttt{layer4},
and the classifier head (\texttt{linear}), indexed 0--5. At $n\!=\!3$ they
are \{stem, \texttt{layer1}\}, \{\texttt{layer2}, \texttt{layer3}\} and
\{\texttt{layer4}, head\}. Rolling back a set of shards replaces all of
their tensors, including BatchNorm statistics, with the infected
versions and leaves every other shard repaired. Rolling back all $n$
shards reproduces the infected model exactly (ASR~$=$~95.06\%,
CA~$=$~91.33\% at both granularities), which verifies the harness.

\subsection{Repair-Share Metric}
\label{sec:share}

The \emph{repair share} of shard $s$ is the fraction of the squared
repair update that it carries:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{share}_s \;=\; \frac{\|\,\delta_s\,\|_2^2}{\sum_j \|\,\delta_j\,\|_2^2},
  \qquad
  \delta_s \;=\; \theta^{\mathrm{rep}}_s - \theta^{\mathrm{inf}}_s,
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
%%Hung: The three \texttt names cannot be hyphenated and leave two badly spaced lines (log: lines 283--290). Replace the text from "BatchNorm buffers" to "not learned." with:
%%Hung: "BatchNorm buffers (running mean, running variance, and batch counter) are excluded, for they are not learned."
where $\theta_s$ are the learnable parameters of shard $s$; BatchNorm
buffers (\texttt{running\_mean}, \texttt{running\_var},
\texttt{num\_batches\_tracked}) are excluded because they are not
learned. By construction $\sum_s \mathrm{share}_s = 1$, and the share of
a set of shards is the sum of their shares. The metric needs only the two
signed checkpoints, so an attacker who holds both can compute it offline.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% Rewritten 26/09 by Hung from results/analysis/2026-09-26_hung_digest.md.

\textbf{Which shards are stale matters more than how many.} A single stale
shard restores at most 16.4\% ASR, so the single-shard case is the lower bound
of the attack. Recovery is not proportional to the repair share reverted:
\texttt{layer3} carries 48\% of the repair update and its rollback alone yields
0.4\% ASR, whereas every hybrid above 50\% ASR contains \texttt{layer4}, which
carries 24\%. The share metric therefore locates the repair but not the
backdoor. An adversary who holds both signed epochs can still exploit it, for
ranking shards by share and rolling back the top four restores 92.3\% and
95.8\% ASR under the two repairs, and an adversary who knows only the partition
needs five.

\textbf{Four stale shards is a stealthy attack.} The four-shard hybrids above
serve at 91.1\% and 91.2\% CA, which is 2.1 and 2.3 points below the repaired
model and within 0.2 points of the infected model that was in service before
the repair. A monitor that compares against the pre-repair baseline sees
nothing; one that compares against the repaired model needs labelled canary
data and an alarm threshold tighter than 2 points. Nothing in the aggregate
accuracy distinguishes the hybrid from the model that was in service before
the repair. Detection therefore belongs at the artifact layer, where
provenance is checked, not at the inference layer, where only aggregate
accuracy is visible.

\textbf{Two repairs, one pattern.} The two fine-tuned repairs differ in seed
and in training normalization, yet their ASR across all 64 subsets correlates
at $r = 0.926$ and they agree on the 50\% classification of 60 subsets. The
four disagreements all contain \texttt{layer4} without \texttt{layer2}, where
the seed-0 repair is the more fragile. Under that repair three hybrids also
exceed the infected model's own ASR, reaching 96.5\%, so a partial rollback can
be a stronger attack than the original model.

\textbf{The fix is cheap.} Every hybrid shares one root cause: each shard is
checked for authenticity, not for membership in the same epoch. Binding every
shard to an epoch identifier in signed snapshot metadata, as TUF~\cite{tuf} and
Uptane~\cite{uptane} specify, rejected every skewed load in our serving test
for 34 extra bytes per manifest. In partitioned 6G edge inference each node
sees only its own shard and resumes an interrupted over-the-air update on its
own, so the epoch check must be enforced per node against a pinned manifest,
not only at the publisher.

\subsection{Limitations}

We study one architecture (PreActResNet18), one dataset (CIFAR-10), one attack
(BadNets at 10\% poisoning), and one family of repair (clean fine-tuning with
the full clean training set, a strong defender), with block-aligned partitions
only. The two repairs differ in seed and, as stated in
Section~\ref{sec:setup}, in training normalization, so they are two
independent repairs rather than a controlled seed study.
\ifincludeanp ANP selects its pruning threshold on test-set ASR, as
BackdoorBench does, which favours the defender. \fi
The serving test uses HMAC-SHA256 as a stand-in for signatures, one skew
scenario at $n=3$, and single-machine timings. The 6G setting enters only
through the deployment model, namely per-shard delivery and verification with
rollouts that an interrupted link leaves partially applied; we do not model
the radio link itself.
\ifincludeanp\else Local repairs, \fi other triggers and architectures, and
non-aligned partitions are future work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Backdoor repair and its fragility.} Fine-tuning and pruning
defenses such as fine-pruning~\cite{liu2018fineprune} and
ANP~\cite{wu2021anp} remove backdoors after training. Min et
al.~\cite{min2024uncovering} show that models purified by several such
defenses, ANP among them, quickly re-learn the
backdoor when retuned on a few poisoned samples or probed with crafted
queries. DeferBad~\cite{shin2024deferbad} designs the backdoor so that
unlearning leaves a residue that later benign fine-tuning reactivates.
%%Hung: The paper says "adversary" everywhere except here and in the next paragraph. Replace "Our attacker" with "Our adversary" in both places.
Both need training or queries. Our attacker needs neither: it restores
backdoor behaviour purely by serving shards that were once legitimately
signed.

\textbf{Weight substitution and merging.} The subnet replacement
attack~\cite{qi2022subnet} overwrites part of a deployed model with
weights trained for the purpose, and BadMerging~\cite{zhang2024badmerging}
plants backdoors through merging of distinct models. Our attacker cannot
craft weights; it reuses the two endpoints of one repair.

\textbf{Artifact integrity and update security.}
Sentry~\cite{gan2025sentry} authenticates ML artifacts as they are loaded,
and the OpenSSF Model Signing specification~\cite{oms2025} signs one
manifest of per-file digests. Both prove that each artifact is authentic.
When shards are verified independently by the nodes that host them, as in
partitioned edge inference~\cite{kang2017neurosurgeon,letaief2022edgeai6g},
authenticity alone does not stop a node from accepting a stale but genuine
shard. TUF~\cite{tuf} and Uptane~\cite{uptane} define the rollback and
mix-and-match protections that close this gap; we quantify what their
absence costs for a repaired model.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Signed but stale shards can undo a backdoor repair in partitioned edge
inference. Against a strong fine-tuning repair, one stale shard restores at
most 16.4\% ASR, but four shards chosen by repair share restore 92.3\% and
95.8\% ASR at a clean accuracy within 2.3 points of the repaired model, and
recovery hinges on \texttt{layer4} being stale rather than on the fraction of
the repair reverted. Two independent repairs agree on this pattern in 60 of 64
rollback subsets.

Epoch-manifest pinning rejects these hybrids for 34 bytes per manifest and
should be a baseline control wherever 6G edge nodes receive model shards
through caches.
