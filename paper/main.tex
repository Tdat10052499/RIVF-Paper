\begin{abstract}
Edge intelligence in 6G networks distributes deep models to edge nodes as
independently signed shards that are cached and updated in stages over
intermittent links. A per-shard signature proves that a shard is authentic,
not that every shard belongs to the same model version. We measure what this
gap costs after a backdoor has been repaired, when an adversary who controls
the artifact cache re-introduces validly signed pre-repair shards into an
otherwise repaired model. Using BadNets on CIFAR-10 with PreActResNet18
partitioned into six shards, we evaluate all 63 rollback subsets against two
independently fine-tuned repairs. Rolling back one shard restores at most
16.4\% attack success rate (ASR). Recovery depends on which shards are stale
rather than on how much of the repair they carry: every hybrid above 50\% ASR
contains \texttt{layer4}, whereas the shard holding 48\% of the repair update
is dispensable. An adversary who ranks shards by repair share restores between
92.3\% and 95.8\% ASR with four stale shards while clean accuracy stays within
2.3 points of the repaired model, and the two repairs agree on the 50\%
classification of 60 of 64 subsets. Epoch-manifest pinning, as specified by
TUF and Uptane, rejects every skewed load for 34 bytes per manifest and no
measurable verification overhead.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Rewritten 26/09 by Hung from results/analysis/2026-09-26_hung_digest.md.

Backdoor repair does not end a model's lifecycle, for the repaired model must
still be distributed, cached, and served. In 6G edge intelligence a model is
increasingly partitioned into independently signed shards, so that edge nodes
can fetch, cache, and verify each shard separately over intermittent wireless
links, and a repair can be rolled out in stages rather than as one atomic
replacement~\cite{kang2017neurosurgeon,letaief2022edgeai6g}. Staged rollout is
attractive precisely because it tolerates interruption: a link outage or a
handover leaves some nodes on the new version and others on the old, and the
deployment continues to serve. Supply-chain protections for this setting verify
that each shard is authentically signed. A per-shard signature, however, proves
only that a shard is a genuine artifact; it does not prove that the shard
belongs to the same repair epoch as the shards around it. An adversary who
controls the artifact cache or the update path, without forging a signature or
compromising a loader, can supply a validly signed pre-repair shard to a node
during an interrupted rollout, and the node then serves a hybrid model that
mixes repaired and stale weights.

We measure the machine-learning consequence of this gap rather than propose a
protocol. Using BadNets on CIFAR-10 with PreActResNet18 partitioned into six
shards, we repair the infected model by clean fine-tuning under two seeds,
evaluate every one of the 63 possible rollback subsets against each repair, and
ask when a hybrid restores backdoor behaviour and whether utility monitoring
would notice. Three findings follow. Rolling back a single shard is weak: the
largest single-shard attack success rate (ASR) is 16.4\%, and the clean
accuracy (CA) of every single-shard hybrid stays within 6.8 points of the
repaired model, so a stale shard is accepted silently and to little effect.
Recovery is governed by which shards are stale rather than by how much of the
repair they carry: every hybrid above 50\% ASR contains \texttt{layer4},
whereas \texttt{layer3}, which holds 48\% of the repair update, is dispensable,
and the repair share covered by a subset predicts its ASR only weakly (Spearman
$\rho \le 0.41$). Finally, an adversary who holds both signed versions can rank
shards by repair share offline; four shards chosen this way restore 92.3\% and
95.8\% ASR under the two repairs, at a CA within 2.3 points of the repaired
model and within 0.2 points of the infected model that was in service before
the repair. The two repairs agree on whether a subset exceeds 50\% ASR in 60 of
64 cases.

The defense we evaluate is deliberately not new. Epoch-manifest pinning binds
every shard to a per-epoch identifier in signed snapshot metadata, so that a
loader rejects a shard as epoch-mismatched instead of accepting it as
individually valid; TUF~\cite{tuf} and Uptane~\cite{uptane} specify it for
exactly this class of problem. Our contribution is to show that the gap it
closes is open in model-shard distribution and to measure what closing it
costs: in our serving comparison (Section~\ref{sec:serving}) it adds 34 bytes
of manifest per epoch and no measurable per-load verification cost, which makes
it a baseline control rather than a design trade-off.

Section~\ref{sec:threat} states the threat model. Section~\ref{sec:setup}
describes the dataset, architecture, attack, repairs, and partitioning.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the single-shard, multi-shard, and
shard-selection results for both repairs, \ifincludeanp the contrast with a
local repair, \fi the determinism and replication checks, and the serving
comparison. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the results and states
their limits, Section~\ref{sec:related} relates them to prior work, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

\textbf{Setting.} A model is partitioned into $n$ contiguous shards that
are hosted by different edge nodes (split or partitioned
inference~\cite{kang2017neurosurgeon}), a deployment pattern envisioned for
6G edge intelligence~\cite{letaief2022edgeai6g}. Each shard is signed
individually and delivered through an artifact cache, and each node
verifies only the shard it hosts. Repairs are rolled out in stages, so
an interrupted rollout caused by a link outage or handover is a normal
operating condition.

\textbf{Adversary.} The adversary controls only the artifact cache or
update path. Its goal is to make the deployment serve a \emph{hybrid}
model that mixes repaired shards with validly signed pre-repair shards,
restoring the backdoor without failing any per-shard check. It knows the
architecture and partition boundaries and holds both signed versions of
every shard, which any party that cached the pre-repair artifacts has.
It can therefore compute, offline, how much of the repair each shard
carries (Section~\ref{sec:share}). It needs no queries to the model and
no training data.

\textbf{Trust assumptions.} The coordinator, loader, runtime and any
stored minimum-epoch state are trusted; signatures cannot be forged.
A fully compromised worker that misreports its loaded weights is out of
scope, since it requires attestation.

Per-shard verification proves that each shard is authentic, not that
all shards belong to the same epoch. Epoch-manifest pinning, as defined
in TUF~\cite{tuf} and Uptane~\cite{uptane}, closes this gap without a new
protocol. We measure the ML-specific consequence of its absence.

\subsection{Models, Attack, and Repairs}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Data, model and attack.} We use CIFAR-10~\cite{krizhevsky2009cifar}
(50{,}000 training and 10{,}000 test images) and
PreActResNet18~\cite{he2016identity}. The infected model is the publicly
released BackdoorBench~\cite{backdoorbench2024} BadNets~\cite{gu2019badnets}
checkpoint for this pair (poisoning ratio 10\%, 3$\times$3 white patch in the
bottom-right corner, target class~0); we do not retrain it. Evaluated under its native input normalization it
reaches CA~$=$~91.33\% and ASR~$=$~95.06\%, matching the published values.

\textbf{Repair~(a): global clean fine-tuning.} The infected model is
fine-tuned for 20 epochs on the full clean training set (SGD, momentum 0.9,
weight decay $5\!\times\!10^{-4}$, learning rate 0.01 with cosine annealing,
batch 128), updating every parameter. We train two such repairs. The seed-42
repair was fine-tuned under a slightly different input scaling (per-channel
standard deviation 0.2023, 0.1994, 0.2010) from the one used for evaluation;
its accuracy changes by less than 0.2 points between the two, so this does not
affect the results. The seed-0 repair uses the evaluation normalization
throughout. They reach CA~$=$~93.18\% and ASR~$=$~0.81\% (seed 42) and
CA~$=$~93.51\% and ASR~$=$~1.13\% (seed 0). Giving the defender the full
clean training set makes this a strong, global repair.
\ifincludeanp

\textbf{Repair~(b): Adversarial Neuron Pruning (ANP).} We apply
ANP~\cite{wu2021anp} to the same infected checkpoint with BackdoorBench's
reference implementation and default CIFAR-10 settings
(Table~\ref{tab:anp_config}). ANP learns a mask score per BatchNorm
neuron on 5\% of the clean training data and prunes (zeroes) every neuron
whose score is at most the selected threshold, leaving all other weights
unchanged. At threshold~\ANPthr{}, \ANPpruned{} of 3{,}392 neurons are
pruned, giving CA~$=$~\ANPca\% and ASR~$=$~\ANPasr\%. We verified that
every other parameter is bit-identical to the infected model, so the two
checkpoints share one lineage, as the threat model requires.

\subsection{Partitioning and Substitution}
\label{sec:partition}

We partition PreActResNet18 into $n$ contiguous shards along
top-level module boundaries. At $n\!=\!6$ the shards are the stem
(\texttt{conv1}), the four residual stages \texttt{layer1}--\texttt{layer4},
and the classifier head (\texttt{linear}), indexed 0--5. At $n\!=\!3$ they
are \{stem, \texttt{layer1}\}, \{\texttt{layer2}, \texttt{layer3}\} and
\{\texttt{layer4}, head\}. Rolling back a set of shards replaces all of
their tensors, including BatchNorm statistics, with the infected
versions and leaves every other shard repaired. Rolling back all $n$
shards reproduces the infected model exactly (ASR~$=$~95.06\%,
CA~$=$~91.33\% at both granularities), which verifies the harness.

\subsection{Repair-Share Metric}
\label{sec:share}

The \emph{repair share} of shard $s$ is the fraction of the squared
repair update that it carries:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{share}_s \;=\; \frac{\|\,\delta_s\,\|_2^2}{\sum_j \|\,\delta_j\,\|_2^2},
  \qquad
  \delta_s \;=\; \theta^{\mathrm{rep}}_s - \theta^{\mathrm{inf}}_s,
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_s$ are the learnable parameters of shard $s$; BatchNorm
buffers (\texttt{running\_mean}, \texttt{running\_var},
\texttt{num\_batches\_tracked}) are excluded because they are not
learned. By construction $\sum_s \mathrm{share}_s = 1$, and the share of
a set of shards is the sum of their shares. The metric needs only the two
signed checkpoints, so an attacker who holds both can compute it offline.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\textbf{ASR} is the fraction of triggered test images whose true class is
not the target that are classified as the target class
(9{,}000 images), following BackdoorBench~\cite{backdoorbench2024}.
\textbf{CA} is top-1 accuracy on the 10{,}000 clean test images. Both are
measured in evaluation mode. All models are evaluated with BackdoorBench's
input normalization (per-channel standard deviation 0.247, 0.243, 0.261).
With these test-set sizes, the 95\%
binomial confidence half-width is at most 1.0 percentage point for
either metric, so we report differences below 1 point as ties.

\subsection{Sanity Checks: Hybrid Models Are Not Broken}

Before evaluating partial rollbacks, we verify that the substitution
harness is correct and that the resulting hybrid models remain functional.

\textbf{Full-rollback reconstruction.} Substituting all $n$ shards with
their pre-repair counterparts should exactly reconstruct the infected model.
Table~\ref{tab:fullrollback} confirms this at both granularities: the
recovered ASR and CA match the infected baseline exactly, ruling out any
bug in the substitution code.

\textbf{Hybrid models are not broken.} Across all partial rollback
configurations ($n=6$, one to five shards substituted), hybrid models
achieve clean accuracies between 74.76\% and 93.15\% (seed~42) and
79.87\% to 93.52\% (seed~0). No hybrid model degrades to random-chance
accuracy ($\approx$10\%). This is important: it means a deployment
that monitors only clean accuracy would observe nothing unusual when a
stale shard is silently accepted.

We adopt 50\% ASR as this study's criterion for a functionally restored
backdoor---the level at which the attack succeeds on a majority of
triggered inputs.
No individual shard rollback restores the backdoor above the 50\%
ASR threshold. Across all six shards, the highest single-shard ASR

distributed across multiple shards in a way that no single shard can

implication: the hybrid model's clean accuracy

repaired model at typical monitoring thresholds. The threat surface
exists; it simply requires multiple stale shards, as
Section~\ref{sec:kcurve} shows.

thus non-linear: the backdoor is not gradually restored proportionally
to coverage, but exhibits a sharp recovery once the repair is
sufficiently undermined.

\subsection{What Drives Recovery: Repair Share and Shard Interaction}
\label{sec:locality}

Fig.~\ref{fig:locality_asr} plots, for every shard subset (all 63 non-empty
subsets at $n\!=\!6$), the repair share it
covers against the ASR it restores. Share alone is a weak predictor
(Spearman $\rho = 0.33$ for seed~42, $\rho = 0.41$ for seed~0,
computed by scripts/plot\_locality\_asr.py). A single shard never exceeds
5.36\% ASR (seed~42) or 16.40\% (seed~0), even
shard~3, which carries 47.74\% of the repair and restores only 0.40\%.
The key structural finding is that \texttt{layer4} is necessary: every hybrid
above 50\% ASR contains \texttt{layer4} in both repairs.
\texttt{layer3}---the shard with the largest repair share (47.7\%)---is
dispensable: the worst-case four-shard rollback under seed~0
($\{1,2,4,5\}$, 96.07\% ASR) excludes it entirely. The share-greedy
attacker of Section~\ref{sec:strategy} discovers these paths because
\texttt{layer2}--\texttt{layer4} are also the three highest-share shards.

\subsection{Determinism and Sampling Variance}
\label{sec:variance}

The repair-aware and architectural orders are deterministic, and
re-running the sweep under a second evaluation seed (0 instead of 42)
reproduced all repeated subset evaluations exactly, so the pipeline has
no hidden randomness. This is a determinism check, not a replication over
repair seeds. Only the exact mean over all $\binom{6}{k}$ subsets varies
between repairs: 13.82\% at $k=3$ (seed~42) and 26.55\% (seed~0). The
difference arises because seed-0 subsets are more likely to contain
\texttt{layer4} (Section~\ref{sec:locality}).
\ifrepairseed
To test whether the result depends on the repair itself, we fine-tuned a
second repaired model with seed~0 (CA~$=$~93.51\%, ASR~$=$~1.13\%,
trained under BackdoorBench normalization throughout).
Its three highest-share shards are the same as seed~42 ($\{3,4,2\}$),
and rolling them back restores 81.11\% ASR at 89.93\% CA\ ($k\!=\!3$); at
$k\!=\!4$ ($\{1,2,3,4\}$), ASR reaches 95.82\% at 91.20\% CA---within
0.13 points of the infected baseline. The two repairs agree on whether a
subset exceeds 50\% ASR in 60 of 64 cases (Pearson $r=0.926$).

\subsection{Deployment and Serving Comparison}
\label{sec:serving}
% Owner: Ho Du Tuan Dat.
All timings below were measured on a single M2 MacBook, on CPU, with
each scheme's loader run as one OS process; this
comparison uses the $n=3$ shard partition, unlike the $n=6$ partition
used elsewhere in the paper. Table~\ref{tab:serving} compares
per-shard signature checking---where ``signature'' denotes
HMAC-SHA256 used here as a stand-in for a production signing
scheme---against epoch-manifest pinning as defenses against
shard-version skew, measured over 30 repetitions per (scheme,
scenario) cell against the real infected/repaired checkpoint pair,
with each scheme's loader run as a separate OS process. In the skewed-load scenario -- one shard substituted
with an older, independently and validly signed version, as a per-shard-
signature-only loader would see after an interrupted rollout -- per-shard
signature checking accepts the substituted shard in all 30 repetitions,
since it has no way to detect that the shard belongs to a different repair
epoch than its neighbours; epoch-manifest pinning rejects it in all 30
repetitions and correctly identifies the substituted shard. This added
protection costs 34 additional bytes of manifest per epoch (848 vs.\ 814
bytes) to store the epoch identifier, and adds no measurable per-load
verification cost once the identifier is hashed via an incremental update
rather than a full-buffer concatenation (132.0~$\pm$~3.8~ms vs.\
133.4~$\pm$~3.1~ms per load, not statistically distinguishable at this
sample size). Recovering from a detected skew -- reloading from the
correct manifest immediately after rejection -- takes 148.4~$\pm$~5.3~ms;
per-shard signature checking has no equivalent recovery path, since it
never detects the skew to begin with.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% Rewritten 26/09 by Hung from results/analysis/2026-09-26_hung_digest.md.

\textbf{Which shards are stale matters more than how many.} A single stale
shard restores at most 16.4\% ASR, so the single-shard case is the lower bound
of the attack. Recovery is not proportional to the repair share reverted:
\texttt{layer3} carries 48\% of the repair update and its rollback alone yields
0.4\% ASR, whereas every hybrid above 50\% ASR contains \texttt{layer4}, which
carries 24\%. The share metric therefore locates the repair but not the
backdoor. An adversary who holds both signed epochs can still exploit it, for
ranking shards by share and rolling back the top four restores 92.3\% and
95.8\% ASR under the two repairs, and an adversary who knows only the partition
needs five.

\textbf{Four stale shards is a stealthy attack.} The four-shard hybrids above
serve at 91.1\% and 91.2\% CA, which is 2.1 and 2.3 points below the repaired
model and within 0.2 points of the infected model that was in service before
the repair. A monitor that compares against the pre-repair baseline sees
nothing; one that compares against the repaired model needs labelled canary
data and an alarm threshold tighter than 2 points. Label-free monitoring would
not detect it. Detection therefore belongs at the artifact layer, where
provenance is checked, not at the inference layer, where only aggregate
accuracy is visible.

\textbf{Two repairs, one pattern.} The two fine-tuned repairs differ in seed
and in training normalization, yet their ASR across all 64 subsets correlates
at $r = 0.926$ and they agree on the 50\% classification of 60 subsets. The
four disagreements all contain \texttt{layer4} without \texttt{layer2}, where
the seed-0 repair is the more fragile. Under that repair three hybrids also
exceed the infected model's own ASR, reaching 96.5\%, so a partial rollback can
be a stronger attack than the original model.

\textbf{The fix is cheap.} Every hybrid shares one root cause: each shard is
checked for authenticity, not for membership in the same epoch. Binding every
shard to an epoch identifier in signed snapshot metadata, as TUF~\cite{tuf} and
Uptane~\cite{uptane} specify, rejected every skewed load in our serving test
for 34 extra bytes per manifest. In partitioned edge inference each node sees
only its own shard, so the epoch check must be enforced per node against a
pinned manifest, not only at the publisher.

\subsection{Limitations}

We study one architecture (PreActResNet18), one dataset (CIFAR-10), one attack
(BadNets at 10\% poisoning), and one family of repair (clean fine-tuning with
the full clean training set, a strong defender), with block-aligned partitions
only. The two repairs differ in seed and, as stated in
Section~\ref{sec:setup}, in training normalization, so they are two
independent repairs rather than a controlled seed study.
\ifincludeanp ANP selects its pruning threshold on test-set ASR, as
BackdoorBench does, which favours the defender. \fi
The serving test uses HMAC-SHA256 as a stand-in for signatures, one skew
scenario at $n=3$, and single-machine timings.
\ifincludeanp\else Local repairs, \fi other triggers and architectures, and
non-aligned partitions are future work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Backdoor repair and its fragility.} Fine-tuning and pruning
defenses such as fine-pruning~\cite{liu2018fineprune} and
ANP~\cite{wu2021anp} remove backdoors after training. Min et
al.~\cite{min2024uncovering} show that models purified by several such
defenses, ANP among them, quickly re-learn the
backdoor when retuned on a few poisoned samples or probed with crafted
queries. DeferBad~\cite{shin2024deferbad} designs the backdoor so that
unlearning leaves a residue that later benign fine-tuning reactivates.
Both need training or queries. Our attacker needs neither: it restores
backdoor behaviour purely by serving shards that were once legitimately
signed.

\textbf{Weight substitution and merging.} The subnet replacement
attack~\cite{qi2022subnet} overwrites part of a deployed model with
weights trained for the purpose, and BadMerging~\cite{zhang2024badmerging}
plants backdoors through merging of distinct models. Our attacker cannot
craft weights; it reuses the two endpoints of one repair.

\textbf{Artifact integrity and update security.}
Sentry~\cite{gan2025sentry} authenticates ML artifacts as they are loaded,
and the OpenSSF Model Signing specification~\cite{oms2025} signs one
manifest of per-file digests. Both prove that each artifact is authentic.
When shards are verified independently by the nodes that host them, as in
partitioned edge inference~\cite{kang2017neurosurgeon,letaief2022edgeai6g},
authenticity alone does not stop a node from accepting a stale but genuine
shard. TUF~\cite{tuf} and Uptane~\cite{uptane} define the rollback and
mix-and-match protections that close this gap; we quantify what their
absence costs for a repaired model.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Signed but stale shards can undo a backdoor repair in partitioned edge
inference. Against a strong fine-tuning repair, one stale shard restores at
most 16.4\% ASR, but four shards chosen by repair share restore 92.3\% to
95.8\% ASR at a clean accuracy within 2.3 points of the repaired model, and
recovery hinges on \texttt{layer4} being stale rather than on the fraction of
the repair reverted. Two independent repairs agree on this pattern in 60 of 64
rollback subsets.

should be a baseline control wherever 6G edge nodes receive model shards
through caches.
