\begin{abstract}
We study shard-version skew as an attack surface in distributed edge
inference: a setting in which a model is partitioned into independently
signed shards that may be cached and served by separate workers during
a rolling update.
An attacker who retains a validly signed but pre-repair shard can
silently introduce it into an otherwise-repaired deployment, producing
a hybrid model without triggering per-shard signature checks.
We measure the ML-specific consequence of this gap using BadNets on
CIFAR-10 with PreActResNet18, partitioned into six shards, after two
representative repairs: global clean fine-tuning and Adversarial
Neuron Pruning (ANP).
Under fine-tuning, single-shard rollback is weak---the highest
single-shard attack success rate (ASR) is 8.94\%---yet completely
invisible to utility monitoring: clean accuracy stays above 86\% in
all hybrid configurations.
A repair-aware attacker substituting the three highest-share shards
restores ASR to 92.89\% while maintaining CA~$=$~87.38\%, a result
that replicates exactly across two independent random seeds.
Under ANP, any single-shard substitution causes utility collapse
(CA~$\approx$~10\%) rather than backdoor recovery, making tampering
detectable through standard accuracy monitoring.
Epoch-manifest pinning---already specified in TUF and Uptane---closes
the attack surface with 34 additional bytes of manifest overhead and
no measurable per-load verification cost.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
backdoor attacks, model partitioning, distributed edge inference,

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Owner: Dan + Dat, together, 25/09.

Backdoor repair is not the end of a model's lifecycle; the repaired
model still has to be distributed, cached, and served. Edge inference
deployments increasingly partition a model into independently signed
shards so that workers can cache, update, and verify them separately
during a rolling update, rather than replacing the entire model
atomically. This partitioning is attractive precisely because it makes
rollout incremental: a repair can reach some workers before others,
and an interrupted or partial rollout is treated as an ordinary
operational condition rather than a failure. Existing supply-chain
protections for this setting verify that each shard is authentically
signed, but per-shard signature verification proves only that a shard
is a genuine artifact, not that it belongs to the same repair epoch as
the shards around it. An adversary who controls the artifact cache or
the update distribution path -- without forging any signature or
compromising the loader -- can exploit exactly this gap, supplying a
validly signed, pre-repair shard to a worker during an interrupted
rollout and causing it to serve a hybrid model that mixes repaired and
stale weights.

We measure the ML-specific consequence of this gap rather than propose
a new protocol. Using BadNets on CIFAR-10 with PreActResNet18,
partitioned into six shards, we ask when a hybrid model assembled this
way restores backdoor behavior, and whether that restoration is
visible to standard utility monitoring. The answer depends sharply on
the repair method. Under global clean fine-tuning, rolling back any
single shard is weak: the highest single-shard attack success rate
(ASR) we observe is 8.94\%, and the repair is distributed across
shards in a way that no individual shard can undo. This weakness is
not benign, however, because it is also silent -- clean accuracy (CA)
stays above 86\% in every hybrid configuration we test, so utility
monitoring gives a defender no signal that a stale shard has been
accepted. We report this single-shard result plainly as the lower
bound of the attack, not as its headline, and place its practical
implication first: detection has to happen at the artifact layer,
where shard provenance is checked, not at the inference layer, where
only aggregate accuracy is visible. The real attack surface appears
once an adversary substitutes multiple shards. A repair-aware attacker
who targets the shards carrying the largest share of the repair update
crosses a sharp threshold at three substituted shards, restoring
ASR to 92.89\% while maintaining CA at 87.38\%, a result that
replicates exactly across two independent random seeds; an attacker
with no knowledge of the repair internals, selecting shards by
architectural position alone, reaches the same threshold with only
two additional shards. Under Adversarial Neuron Pruning (ANP), the
picture reverses: any single-shard substitution collapses utility
(CA~$\approx$~10\%) before the backdoor can recover, because the
pruning mask ANP bakes into the retained shards is architecturally
incompatible with the full weights of a pre-repair shard. This makes
tampering self-revealing under an ANP repair, in contrast to the
fine-tuned case.

The defense we evaluate is deliberately not novel. Epoch-manifest
pinning -- binding each shard to a per-epoch identifier in signed
snapshot metadata, so that a loader can reject a shard as
epoch-mismatched rather than accept it as individually valid -- is
already specified in update frameworks such as TUF~\cite{tuf} and
Uptane~\cite{uptane} for exactly this class of problem. Our
contribution is to show that this gap exists, unaddressed, in
model-shard distribution, and to measure what closing it costs: our
serving comparison (Section~\ref{sec:serving}) finds 34 additional
bytes of manifest overhead and no measurable per-load verification
cost, making adoption a low-cost baseline control rather than a
design trade-off.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:threat} formalizes the threat model.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the dataset, architecture, attack,
repairs, and partitioning scheme.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the single-shard, multi-shard, and
ANP-contrast results together with a second-seed replication and the
serving comparison.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets these results as two distinct
vulnerability regimes and situates epoch-manifest pinning as their
common remedy, and states the scope limitations of our measurements.
Section~\ref{sec:related} relates this work to prior model
partitioning, backdoor, and supply-chain security literature, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\subsection{Threat Model}
\label{sec:threat}

We consider an adversary whose sole point of control is the artifact
cache or model-update distribution path of an edge inference service.
The attacker's objective is to cause at least one worker to serve a
\emph{hybrid} model---one composed of a mix of repaired shards and a
historically valid but pre-repair shard---thereby restoring backdoor
behavior without triggering per-shard signature checks.

\textbf{Attacker knowledge.} The adversary knows the model architecture
and the shard partition boundaries. Critically, the attacker possesses
at least one shard that was validly signed under a prior, pre-repair
version of the model. Such a shard is trivially available to any
participant in the artifact pipeline who recorded the signed binaries
before the repair was deployed.

\textbf{Attacker control.} The attacker controls only the artifact cache
or update path. They can supply a historically valid, correctly signed
shard to a worker during an interrupted rollout---for instance, when
intermittent connectivity causes some workers to receive newly repaired
shards while others receive cached, pre-repair copies.

\textbf{Attacker budget.} No query budget or byte budget applies; the
attack requires only a single well-timed shard substitution during
rollout interruption. The attacker does not need to interact with the
model at inference time.

\textbf{Trusted components.} The coordinator, loader, runtime, and any
persistently stored minimum-epoch state are trusted. The attacker
cannot forge shard signatures or cause the loader to execute
undeclared weights.

\textbf{Out of scope.} We do not consider a fully compromised worker
that misrepresents its currently loaded weights (that requires
verifiable computation or attestation and is orthogonal to our
setting), nor do we consider forgery of signatures or compromise of
the trusted loader or runtime.

This threat model captures a realistic gap in current deployment
practice: per-shard signature verification proves that each shard is
authentic, but does not prove that all shards belong to the same model
epoch. Epoch-manifest pinning, as defined in TUF~\cite{tuf} and
Uptane~\cite{uptane}, closes this gap without requiring a new protocol.
The contribution of this paper is to measure the ML-specific
consequence of its absence.

\subsection{Models, Attack, and Repairs}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Dataset.} We use CIFAR-10~\cite{krizhevsky2009cifar,backdoorbench2024}, comprising
50{,}000 training images and 10{,}000 test images across 10 classes
(32$\times$32 pixels, RGB). All experiments use the standard train/test
split with no additional augmentation beyond what BackdoorBench's
preprocessing pipeline applies.

\textbf{Model.} We use PreActResNet18~\cite{he2016identity,backdoorbench2024}, a
variant of ResNet-18 with pre-activation residual blocks, as our
target architecture ($\approx$11M parameters). This is the standard
CIFAR-10 benchmark model in BackdoorBench and admits a natural shard
partition along residual block boundaries.

\textbf{Backdoor attack.} We apply BadNets~\cite{gu2019badnets,backdoorbench2024}
with a 3$\times$3 white-pixel trigger patch placed at the bottom-right
corner of each poisoned image (pixels $[29{:}32,\,29{:}32,\,:]$ set
to 255 in uint8 representation). The target class is 0 (``airplane'').
The poison rate is 10\% of the training set. The infected checkpoint
was produced using BackdoorBench's standard training pipeline
(random seed 42), yielding an infected model with CA~$=$~89.41\% and
ASR~$=$~97.43\% on the CIFAR-10 test set.

\textbf{Repair~(a): global clean fine-tune.} The infected model is
fine-tuned for 20 epochs on the clean (unmodified) CIFAR-10 training
set. Optimizer: SGD with momentum~0.9, weight decay~$5\!\times\!10^{-4}$,
initial learning rate~0.01, schedule: CosineAnnealingLR with
$T_{\max}=20$, random seed 42. This repair is global in the sense
that every parameter is updated during fine-tuning. The resulting
repaired model achieves CA~$=$~93.35\% and ASR~$=$~0.90\%.

\textbf{Repair~(b): Adversarial Neuron Pruning (ANP).} We apply ANP
using BackdoorBench's reference
implementation~\cite{backdoorbench2024}, without modification. ANP is a \emph{local} repair: it prunes
neurons identified as backdoor-sensitive by adversarial mask
optimization, leaving the rest of the model's weights unchanged. The
exact configuration used is given in
Table~\ref{tab:anp_config}. ANP is the primary local-repair baseline
against which the shard-rollback attack is evaluated; its locality
makes it the expected worst-case repair from the attacker's perspective
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).
The ANP-repaired checkpoint achieves CA~$=$~84.22\% and ASR~$=$~0.00\%
at pruning threshold~0.55 (404 of 3{,}392 neurons pruned;
Table~\ref{tab:anp_baseline}). The infected model for this run
(seed 42, same pipeline) yielded CA~$=$~91.71\% and ASR~$=$~94.26\%.

\subsection{Partitioning and Substitution}
\label{sec:partition}

We partition PreActResNet18 into $n$ contiguous shards along residual
block boundaries. We evaluate two granularities: $n\!=\!3$ (coarse)
and $n\!=\!6$ (fine).

For $n\!=\!3$, the three shards are: \textbf{S1}~=~stem + \texttt{layer1}
(first residual block pair); \textbf{S2}~=~\texttt{layer2} +
\texttt{layer3}; \textbf{S3}~=~\texttt{layer4} + classification head.

For $n\!=\!6$, each of the four residual blocks
(\texttt{layer1}--\texttt{layer4}) becomes a separate shard, with the
stem and classification head each forming an additional shard.

The substitution procedure replaces the parameters of the specified shard
in an otherwise-repaired model with weights from the pre-repair (infected)
checkpoint, leaving all other shards untouched. Correctness of the substitution harness was
independently verified by full rollback: substituting \emph{all} $n$
shards reconstructs the infected model exactly, recovering
ASR~$=$~97.43\% and CA~$=$~89.41\% (see Table~\ref{tab:fullrollback}).

\subsection{Repair-Share Metric}
\label{sec:share}

To characterize how much of the repair update is carried by each
shard, we define the \emph{repair-share} of shard $s$ as:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{share}_s \;=\; \frac{\|\,\delta_s\,\|_2^2}{\|\,\delta\,\|_2^2},
  \qquad
  \delta_s \;=\; \theta^{\mathrm{rep}}_s - \theta^{\mathrm{inf}}_s,
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
where $\theta^{\mathrm{rep}}_s$ and $\theta^{\mathrm{inf}}_s$ are the
repaired and infected parameters belonging to shard $s$, and $\delta$
is the concatenation of all $\delta_s$ across all shards.
Because the squared $\ell_2$ norm of a concatenation equals the sum of
squared $\ell_2$ norms of its parts, we have
$\|\delta\|_2^2 = \sum_s \|\delta_s\|_2^2$, so by construction
$\sum_s \mathrm{share}_s = 1$.

\textbf{Important correction.} An earlier version of this metric
(computed prior to 21/09/2026) summed over \emph{all state-dict keys},
including the BatchNorm buffer \texttt{num\_batches\_tracked}. This
counter increments by one per training batch and reaches approximately
7{,}820 after 20 fine-tuning epochs (391 batches/epoch
$\times$~20~epochs), while the actual weight differences are on the
order of 0.5. The counter therefore dominated the squared-norm sum and
caused every shard to appear to carry an identical 25\% of the repair
update---a spurious result that reflected only how many BatchNorm layers
each shard contained, not how much of the repair it actually carried.
The conclusion from the 19/09 note that ``each residual block holds
25\% of the repair'' is withdrawn.

The corrected metric skips all non-learnable buffers:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  \mathrm{SKIP} = \{\;&\texttt{num\_batches\_tracked},\;
    \texttt{running\_mean},\\
  &\texttt{running\_var}\;\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:skip}
\end{equation}
Only tensors whose keys do \emph{not} end with any element of
$\mathrm{SKIP}$ are included in Equation~(\ref{eq:share}). All
$\mathrm{share}_s$ values reported in this paper use the corrected
computation.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\textbf{Attack Success Rate (ASR).} The fraction of \emph{eligible}
triggered test examples classified as the target class (class~0):
\[
  \mathrm{ASR} \;=\;
  \frac{|\{x : \hat{y}(x \oplus t) = c_{\mathrm{tgt}},\;
           y(x) \neq c_{\mathrm{tgt}}\}|}
       {|\{x : y(x) \neq c_{\mathrm{tgt}}\}|},
\]
where $x \oplus t$ denotes applying the BadNets trigger to image $x$,
$\hat{y}(\cdot)$ is the model's predicted class, $y(x)$ is the ground
truth, and $c_{\mathrm{tgt}} = 0$. Examples that already belong to the
target class are excluded from both numerator and denominator, following
the standard BackdoorBench evaluation protocol~\cite{backdoorbench2024}.

\textbf{Clean Accuracy (CA).} Standard top-1 accuracy on the
unmodified CIFAR-10 test set (10{,}000 images, no trigger applied).

Both metrics are evaluated in \texttt{model.eval()} mode with batch
normalization running statistics fixed. The Day-5 gate thresholds
established in the Project Agreement were: ASR~$\geq$~80\% for the
infected model (passed: 97.43\%), ASR~$\leq$~10\% for the repaired
model (passed: 0.90\%), and a post-rollback ASR increase of at
least~50\% or at least~30 percentage points over the repaired model
for the single-shard experiment (not met: best single-shard
ASR~$=$~17.46\%). The last result is a genuine negative finding and is
reported as such throughout (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Sanity Checks: Hybrid Models Are Not Broken}

Before evaluating partial rollbacks, we verify that the substitution
harness is correct and that the resulting hybrid models remain functional.

\textbf{Full-rollback reconstruction.} Substituting all $n$ shards with
their pre-repair counterparts should exactly reconstruct the infected model.
Table~\ref{tab:fullrollback} confirms this at both granularities: the
recovered ASR and CA match the infected baseline exactly, ruling out any
bug in the substitution code.

\textbf{Hybrid models are not broken.} Across all partial rollback
configurations evaluated in this paper (one to five shards substituted,
both $n=3$ and $n=6$), the hybrid models achieve clean accuracies
between 84.58\% and 93.26\%. No hybrid model degrades to random-chance
accuracy ($\approx$10\%). This is important: it means a deployment
that monitors only clean accuracy would observe nothing unusual when a
stale shard is silently accepted.

No individual shard rollback restores the backdoor above the 50\%

cherry-picking configurations. However, we emphasize the stealth
implication: the hybrid model's clean accuracy
(Table~\ref{tab:fullrollback}) remains indistinguishable from a normal
repaired model at typical monitoring thresholds. The threat surface
exists; it simply requires multiple stale shards, as
Section~\ref{sec:kcurve} shows.

\subsection{Contrast with a Local Repair (ANP)}
\label{sec:anp}

The ANP-repaired checkpoint achieves $\text{CA}=83.03\%$ and
$\text{ASR}=0.00\%$ at $k=0$, a stronger initial repair than global
fine-tuning ($\text{ASR}=0.90\%$).
However, the $k$-shard rollback behaviour is qualitatively different.

\paragraph{Utility collapse at $k=1$.}
Any single-shard substitution drops clean accuracy to approximately
$10\%$---the level expected from a uniformly random classifier on
CIFAR-10---in each of the four shards tested individually.
The mechanism is architectural: ANP bakes its pruning mask into the
weight tensors by zeroing pruned neurons in the checkpoint.
When an infected shard carrying full weights is spliced next to
ANP shards whose neurons are zeroed, the activation signal becomes
incompatible with the remainder of the network, causing the model
to collapse to a single predicted class.

\paragraph{High ASR as a measurement artifact.}
At $k=1$ when shard~5 (the linear classification head) is substituted,
we observe $\text{ASR}=81.10\%$ alongside $\text{CA}=10.01\%$.
This is not evidence of backdoor reactivation.
The collapsed model predicts class~0 (the attack target,
\textit{airplane}) for every input; the ASR metric registers high
success because the trigger also targets class~0.
Unlike fine-tuned hybrids, which retain high clean accuracy while
recovering ASR proportionally to the number of substituted shards,
ANP hybrids collapse to random-chance clean accuracy at $k=1$.
This utility collapse precedes any potential backdoor recovery,
making shard substitution on ANP-defended models detectable through
standard accuracy monitoring rather than trigger-based evaluation.

\paragraph{Harness verification.}
At $k=6$ (full rollback), both repair strategies correctly reconstruct
the original infected model: $\text{ASR}=97.43\%$, $\text{CA}=89.41\%$.
This confirms that the evaluation harness is correct and that the
collapse at $k<6$ is caused by the hybrid architecture, not by a
pipeline error.

\subsection{Second-Seed Replication}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results reveal two qualitatively distinct vulnerability regimes
under shard-version skew, determined by the repair method.

\paragraph{Single-shard rollback is weak but silent.}
Under global fine-tuning, no individual shard rollback restores the
backdoor above 9\% ASR, even when the substituted shard carries nearly
half of the total repair update ($\sigma_3{=}47.74\%$,
ASR~$=$~0.50\%).
The repair is distributed across the network in a way that no single
shard can undo.
Critically, this weakness does not make the threat benign: hybrid
models retain CA above 86\% throughout, so standard utility monitoring
provides no signal that a stale shard has been accepted.
The threat requires detection at the artifact layer, not the inference
layer.

\paragraph{Coordinated multi-shard rollback is the real attack
surface.}
A repair-aware attacker who substitutes the three highest-share shards
crosses a sharp threshold (ASR~$=$~92.89\% at $k{=}3$, up from
4.57\% at $k{=}2$), matching the fully infected model's 97.43\%
within one further shard.
Architectural selection---requiring only knowledge of the partition
boundaries, not of the repair update---crosses the same threshold at
$k{=}5$, confirming that the attack does not require privileged
knowledge of repair internals.

\paragraph{ANP creates a self-revealing collapse regime.}
Shard substitution on an ANP-repaired model destroys utility before
the backdoor can recover.
The architectural incompatibility between the baked-in pruning mask
and the full weights of a pre-repair shard makes tampering immediately
detectable through CA monitoring alone---a favorable signal for
defenders---but both repair strategies are fully vulnerable to
complete rollback at $k{=}6$.

\paragraph{Epoch-manifest pinning closes the gap.}
Both vulnerability regimes share the same root cause: the artifact
pipeline enforces that each shard is authentic but not that all shards
belong to the same repair epoch.
Epoch-manifest pinning, as defined in TUF~\cite{tuf} and
Uptane~\cite{uptane}, binds each shard to a per-epoch identifier in
the signed snapshot metadata; a loader that checks this identifier
rejects a stale shard as epoch-mismatched rather than accepting it as
individually valid.
Our serving comparison (Section~\ref{sec:serving}) confirms that this
protection adds 34 bytes of manifest overhead and no measurable
per-load verification cost.
The defense is an application of existing supply-chain controls to a
previously unmeasured ML-specific gap.

\subsection{Limitations}

This study evaluates a single architecture (PreActResNet18) on a
single dataset (CIFAR-10) with a single attack pattern (BadNets,
visible patch trigger, 10\% poison rate) and two repair methods;
fine-pruning, Neural Attention Distillation, short-epoch fine-tuning,
other architectures (ResNet-50, ViT), larger datasets, non-patch
triggers (blended, WaNet), and non-block-aligned shard partitions
are left as explicit future work.

Similarly, the serving comparison (Section~\ref{sec:serving}) measures
a single skew scenario---one substituted shard out of six, against one
hardware/software configuration---and reports verify-time and
recovery-time as single-run, exploratory measurements rather than a
claim generalizable across manifest sizes, partition granularities, or
deployment environments; only the mechanism's deterministic properties
(accept/reject behavior, manifest-size overhead) were predeclared as
confirmatory targets.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% Owner: Chinh. Filled 23/09/2026.
% NOTE: \cite{airs} identity unconfirmed -- see references.bib comment.
%   Verify with Dr. Hung before the 26/09 freeze.

Our work sits at the intersection of backdoor repair fragility,
weight-level substitution attacks, and artifact supply-chain security.
We survey the six closest lines of prior work and state precisely where
each differs from our contribution.

\textbf{Backdoor repair fragility.}
Min et al.~\cite{min2024uncovering} demonstrate that state-of-the-art
backdoor defenses---including fine-pruning, Neural Cleanse, and
SCALE-UP---leave a latent backdoor signal in the repaired model that
can be reactivated by adversarial retuning or by querying the model
with carefully chosen inputs.
Our work is complementary but distinct: we do not retune or query the
repaired model; instead, we substitute a historically valid, correctly
signed shard from the pre-repair checkpoint, testing whether
\emph{rollback} alone---with no access to training data or
gradients---suffices to reactivate the backdoor.
Shin and Park~\cite{shin2024deferbad} (DeferBad) take a different angle
by engineering the backdoor injection itself so that the unlearning step
leaves a fragile residual that a subsequent \emph{benign} fine-tune
can reactivate, thereby defeating the defense after it has been applied.
Our attack does not require any forward update: the stale shard is a
historically valid artifact that pre-dates the repair, so no
fine-tuning---benign or adversarial---is involved.

\textbf{Weight-level substitution attacks.}
Qi et al.~\cite{qi2022subnet} introduce the Subnet Replacement Attack
(SRA), in which a gray-box attacker freely replaces a contiguous
subnetwork of a victim model with backdoored weights crafted for that
purpose.
The key distinction is attacker freedom: SRA allows arbitrary parameter
selection and a dedicated backdoor-training step, whereas our attacker
is restricted to shards that were already produced, validly signed, and
archived before the repair---a strictly weaker capability that reflects
realistic artifact-cache compromise.
Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024badmerging} (BadMerging) show that backdoors
can survive or be injected through model merging across \emph{distinct}
model lineages using task-vector arithmetic.
Our setting concerns partial rollback \emph{within a single, known model
lineage}, not merging across sources; the merged model in BadMerging
need not ever have passed a repair, whereas our hybrid is constructed
from the repair's own two endpoints.

\textbf{Artifact integrity and deployment security.}
\cite{airs} addresses per-shard hash and signature verification and
loader integrity in distributed ML serving, establishing that each
loaded shard is authentic.
However, per-shard signature verification proves only that a shard
matches a known signed binary; it does not prove that all shards belong
to the \emph{same repair epoch}.
A pre-repair shard that was validly signed before the repair is accepted
by per-shard checking without any indication of version skew---precisely
the gap our work quantifies.
TUF~\cite{tuf} and Uptane~\cite{uptane} address rollback and
mix-and-match attacks at the snapshot-metadata level in software update
systems, and epoch-manifest pinning---as applied in our serving
comparison (Section~\ref{sec:serving})---is a direct application of
these existing controls.
Our contribution is not a new protocol: we measure the ML-specific
consequence of the absence of epoch pinning in a model-shard deployment,
providing the empirical justification for applying TUF/Uptane-style
controls to this setting.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We measured the attack surface created by shard-version skew in
distributed edge inference, where a validly signed but pre-repair
shard can be silently introduced into an otherwise-repaired deployment.
Against a globally fine-tuned model partitioned into six shards,
single-shard rollback is too weak to restore the backdoor
(max ASR~$=$~8.94\%) yet completely invisible to utility monitoring,
while repair-aware three-shard rollback recovers
ASR~$=$~92.89\% at CA~$=$~87.38\%---a result that replicates exactly
across two random seeds.
Against an ANP-repaired model, any single-shard substitution causes
utility collapse (CA~$\approx$~10\%) before the backdoor can recover,
making tampering detectable through standard accuracy monitoring.
Epoch-manifest pinning---already specified in TUF and Uptane---eliminates
the attack surface by binding each shard to a repair epoch, adding
only 34 bytes of manifest overhead and no measurable per-load cost,
and should be adopted as a baseline control wherever model shards are
distributed through artifact caches.
